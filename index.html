What information counts?

Automated systems do not simply “look at everything.” People decide what information enters the system and how much influence different information has.

Plain language: A weight is simply how much influence one piece of information has on a result. A threshold is the cutoff where the system changes what happens next.

Each person's score = (their first number × its weight) + (their second number × its weight). Anyone at or above the cutoff gets flagged.

Optional reflection: What is one piece of information about the people you serve that should never become a number in a system like this? Why that one?

Practice Scan connection: this is the "Choice, privacy, and data" question - what information is used, and who decided it counts?

Follow the decision

An automated result is rarely the end of the story. Trace how information becomes an output, how a person acts on it, and whether anyone can question or correct the result.

Reveal the path one step at a time. At each step, pause and think about what could go wrong or who could question it before you move to the next.

1. Information recorded
2. System interprets it
3. Output appears
4. Human action
5. Correction or appeal

Questions

    Optional reflection: In a setting you know, who has the authority to override a system's output - on paper? And who actually does it in practice? Are those the same person?

    Practice Scan connection: this is the "Human review and correction" question - who can question, correct, or override the system, and what happens when it is wrong?

    Pocket Questions: Eight Things to Ask About Any System

    These are the questions this whole lab practices, in one place. You do not need any technical background to ask them - and you are allowed to ask them about any system that affects your work or the people you serve. Print this page and take it with you.

    1. What is this system actually doing?

      Is it following preset rules, turning data into a score, generating new text, or is a person doing this step? "AI" is not one thing.

    2. What information does it use?

      And just as important: what information about people never becomes an input at all?

    3. Who decided what counts, and how much it counts?

      Someone chose the inputs, the influence each one has, and the cutoff. Those were human decisions.

    4. Could the same data point mean different things for different people?

      A missed appointment can mean disengagement, a lost bus route, a double shift, or a caregiving emergency. The system sees one number.

    5. Do people know the system is being used?

      Can they decline it, ask questions about it, or ask for a person instead?

    6. Who can question, correct, or override the result?

      On paper - and in practice, with the time and authority they actually have.

    7. Whose circumstances could the system misread?

      Language, disability, culture, family structure, work schedules, digital access, history with services.

    8. Does this problem actually need technology?

      Or would staffing, policy change, a simpler tool, or no tool address it better?

    These questions align with the areas your Trauma-Informed Technology Practice Scan asks you to examine.
